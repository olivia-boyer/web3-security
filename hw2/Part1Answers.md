1.	Section 2 describes transactions as a "chain of digital signatures." Explain why digital signatures alone are not sufficient to prevent someone from spending the same coin twice. What additional mechanism is needed?
Signatures work to prove that the coin is being spent by the person who actually owns it. If the coin owner is malicious and tries to double spend, they can simply sign a second transaction. To prevent this, a “Timestamp Server,” or more commonly called the blockchain is needed to verify the history and order of transactions. Along with the blockchain, at least in our implementation, a UTXO pool is created that stores all spendable coin instances. If a coin is not in the UTXO pool, then it cannot be spent.
2.	Section 3 describes linking blocks together using hashes. If an attacker modifies a transaction in an old block, explain step by step why this modification is detectable.
The hash of each block is computed using the hash of the previous block (in our examples through concatenating the old hash to the new data). Thus, when recalculating the hash of the lates block to check integrity, the hash of each block is recursively calculated and incorporated so that the final outputted hash recalculation will appear differerent than the one stored, exposing any modification.
3.	Section 9 describes transactions with multiple inputs and outputs. Suppose Alice has two unspent outputs worth 3 coins and 4 coins, and she wants to pay Bob 5 coins. Describe the inputs and outputs of this transaction, including any change.
There will be two inputs: Alice’s 3-coin unspent output and Alice’s 4-coin unspent output.
There will be two outputs: a 5-coin output sent to Bob and a 2-coin output sent back to Alice
